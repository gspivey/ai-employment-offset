\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

% Role: restate the question, the answer, and what to watch for as AI diffuses.

This paper asks under what conditions AI-induced firm formation can offset the labor that large incumbents shed as they adopt AI.
We model the AI shock as labor-substituting technology at adopters, lower entry costs, and possibly more productive entrants, in a partial-equilibrium firm-dynamics model with a fixed wage.
Every result is a change in labor demand, not in equilibrium employment, and the model is not calibrated.

\paragraph{Main findings.}
At a single firm, whether AI raises or lowers headcount is the Hicks--Marshall comparison of the labor--AI substitution elasticity with the scale elasticity.
In the span-of-control model the threshold is $1/(1-\alpha)$, which is about 2.78 at $\alpha = 0.64$ but rises steeply with $\alpha$, so the regime an economy is in depends on returns to scale as much as on the elasticity.
Once the size of the AI cost shock is held fixed as the elasticity varies, the net labor-demand effect declines monotonically in the elasticity.
Offset fails when substitution is high and AI-native entrants are no more productive than earlier entrants; it can survive high substitution only if entrants are substantially more productive.
Cutting entry costs moves aggregate labor demand little in any specification.
The high-substitution plateau reported in earlier drafts was an artifact of holding the AI price fixed.

In Census business applications, Information and Professional services pulled away from other sectors from March 2025, with no earlier pre-trend, mostly among applicants unlikely to hire, while applications with planned wages fell in every sector.
That pattern fits more but smaller entrants, and it also fits displaced workers registering as contractors.
It depends on the choice of exposed sectors (adding Finance roughly halves it) and on one small comparison sector, and we give it no causal reading.

\paragraph{What to watch.}
The regime map identifies two quantities: labor demand at AI-adopting firms relative to comparable non-adopters, which bears on whether adopters are above or below the threshold, and the productivity and employment of AI-native entrants.
Establishment-level surveys such as the Business Trends and Outlook Survey and the Community Survey on ICT Usage, linked to administrative data on firm entry and growth, can track both as AI diffuses.

\paragraph{What is not done.}
The model does not yet close the labor and product markets, is not calibrated, classifies firms by current rather than pre-shock size, and runs its Monte Carlo with one seed per draw; the cloud-era exposure design and the continuous-exposure business-formation analysis have not been estimated (\cref{sec:disc-limitations}).
Until they are, the paper's contribution is a framework and a conditional map of where offset holds and fails, not an estimate of whether it will.
