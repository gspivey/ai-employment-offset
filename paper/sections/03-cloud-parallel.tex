\section{Motivation: The Cloud Computing Era, 2006--2020}\label{sec:cloud}

% Role: motivation only. These facts do not discipline the AI priors.

\subsection{Why cloud computing is a natural comparison}\label{sec:cloud-why}

Before commercial cloud services, a new technology firm had to buy and operate its own servers before it could test whether its product had demand.
The public launch of Amazon Web Services' storage and compute services in 2006 allowed firms to rent computing capacity by the unit instead, converting a fixed, sunk investment into a variable cost.
\citet{ewens2018cost} exploit this change and show that lower costs of experimentation altered how venture investors financed early-stage firms in the affected sectors, and \citet{jin2017economies} relate cloud adoption to the capital required to launch a firm and to the survival of young establishments.
Using firm-level data, \citet{destefano2023cloud} find that cloud adoption raised employment at young firms.
In the model of \cref{sec:theory}, this shock maps most naturally into a reduction of the entry cost $c_e$ and the fixed operating cost $c_f$.
That is why the cloud era is a natural comparison for the entry-cost component of the AI shock.
We use it only as motivation: the facts below describe what happened, and the replication exercise in \cref{sec:cloud-mechanism} shows that the model does not reproduce them through the channel the analogy is about.

\subsection{Data}\label{sec:cloud-data}

For the United States we use the Census Bureau's Business Dynamics Statistics (BDS), which report firm and establishment entry, exit, and employment by firm size and age; we cite them as \citet{bds2021}, but the figures below come from the release covering 1978--2023 (\texttt{data/cloud\_era\_baseline.md}).
The Business Formation Statistics (BFS) track business applications and planned-wage intentions from 2004 \citep{bfs2026}; we use the vintage released on November 12, 2026, with data through October 2026.
European comparisons, in \cref{app:europe}, use Eurostat business demography statistics and the OECD DynEmp database.

\subsection{What happened: concentration, not offset}\label{sec:cloud-findings}

Contrary to the hypothesis that lower fixed costs would proliferate small firms, US employment became more concentrated over the cloud era \citep{bds2021}:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Small-firm employment share fell}: from 50.9\% (2006) to 47.0\% (2019) for firms with fewer than 500 employees, and about 90\% of the net rise in employment over the period occurred at firms with 500 or more employees.
  \item \textbf{The startup rate declined}: the employer startup rate fell from 10.3\% (2006) to 8.5\% (2019), and the employment share of young firms (five years or younger) fell from 13.4\% to 10.6\%, continuing a secular decline that predates cloud computing \citep{decker2016declining} and that has been linked to slowing labor-force growth \citep{karahan2024demographic,hopenhayn2022population}.
  \item \textbf{The largest firms gained share}: firms with 10,000 or more employees raised their share of employment from 26.3\% to 29.2\%, consistent with the rise of superstar firms documented by \citet{autor2020fall}.
  \item \textbf{Formation shifted to the nonemployer margin}: business applications rose 33.8\% between 2006 and 2019, while applications with planned wages fell 48.1\% \citep{bfs2026}.
\end{itemize}

The most cloud-exposed sector is a partial exception.
The startup rate in Information (NAICS 51) recovered from 8.5\% in 2010 to 11.3\% in 2019, above its 2006 level of 10.6\%, whereas professional services, retail, and manufacturing remained below their 2006 levels \citep{bds2021}.
These aggregate facts are not evidence that cloud computing caused concentration: demographics, regulation, and the forces that favor large firms in intangible-intensive industries \citep{deridder2024market,aghion2023falling} moved over the same years, and firm-level evidence points to cloud adoption helping young firms grow \citep{destefano2023cloud}.

\subsection{Can the model reproduce the cloud era?}\label{sec:cloud-mechanism}

We asked which parameter configurations of the model reproduce the cloud-era pattern of rising concentration and falling dynamism.
In this exercise the AI block stands in for cloud and IT: $\sigma$ denotes the elasticity of substitution between labor and the rented IT input inside an adopting firm (the same parameter as $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ in \cref{sec:ai-ces}), and the adopter's unit-cost saving is held at its baseline value when $\sigma$ changes (\texttt{data/cloud\_replication\_scenarios.md}).
No single entry-side parameter does it: varying the entry cost, the fixed cost, entrant quality, or the founder pool on its own leaves the small-firm share rising.
The best-fitting configuration (\cref{tab:cloud-results}) combines a scale-effect-dominant technology ($\sigma = 2.0$, below $\theta \approx 2.78$ at $\alpha = 0.64$) with entry headwinds: a 20\% \emph{rise} in the employer entry cost, a 20\% smaller founder pool, and no productivity gain for new entrants.

This result cuts against using the cloud era to discipline the AI shock.
The cloud analogy is about a fall in entry costs, yet the configuration that matches the data raises them.
With the cloud-like 20\% cut in the entry cost instead, the startup rate falls by only 0.33 percentage points against 1.8 in the data (\texttt{data/cloud\_replication\_scenarios.md}, section~5).
In the best fit, the technology parameter drives the size distribution and the entry-side headwinds drive the decline in dynamism, so the replication says more about the forces behind declining dynamism \citep{akcigit2021ten,karahan2024demographic} than about cheaper computing.
Because the model's pre-shock levels are far from the data (\cref{sec:sim-parameters}), the comparison is in changes rather than levels.
We therefore do not use the cloud era to set any AI prior.

\subsection{An empirical design for the cloud era}\label{sec:cloud-strategy}

We have not estimated the causal effect of cloud computing on entry.
A design for doing so would compare industries by their ex ante exposure,
\begin{equation}\label{eq:cloud-event}
  y_{jt} = a_j + \lambda_t + \sum_{k \neq -1} \beta_k \, \bigl( \text{Exposure}_j \times \mathbf{1}\{t = 2006 + k\} \bigr) + X_{jt}'\gamma + \varepsilon_{jt},
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes industries, $y_{jt}$ is an outcome such as the firm entry rate or the employment share of firms below 500 employees, and $\text{Exposure}_j$ measures pre-2006 reliance on computing infrastructure (for example, IT capital intensity from the BEA).
Because the aggregate US startup rate declined over this period \citep{decker2016declining}, identification would come from differences across industries relative to a common trend.
This estimation is part of the ongoing work listed in \cref{sec:disc-limitations}.
