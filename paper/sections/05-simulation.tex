\subsection{Parameters and the baseline economy}\label{sec:sim-parameters}

The model is \emph{not calibrated}.
Its parameters (\cref{tab:calibration}) are illustrative defaults, hand-set to give an entry rate near 7\% and a right-skewed firm size distribution; none was estimated, and the simulated method of moments estimator in the code is a stub.
\Cref{tab:moments} compares the pre-AI economy, simulated for five seeds, with US data.
The model's entry rate is close to the data, but it puts a quarter of employment in firms with 500 or more employees against a little over half in the data, has too few very small firms, and has larger firms on average.
Its Pareto tail for firms with 100 or more employees is thinner than a Zipf law would imply, though we have no data target for that cutoff.
The simulated economy is also small: about 5,700 firms and 180,000 workers, with about 41 firms of 500 or more employees and between zero and three of 5,000 or more in the final cross-section of each seed.
Levels are therefore not comparable with the data, and the quantitative results below are statements about this parameterization, not about the US economy.

\subsection{Simulation design}\label{sec:sim-design}

For each parameter vector, we simulate the economy for 40 periods (AI diffuses from period 5) and compare it with a no-AI counterfactual run from the same burned-in economy and the same random-number seed, so that each surviving firm receives the same productivity and exit shocks in both runs.
Outcomes are averaged over the last 10 periods.
The wage is fixed and labor supply perfectly elastic (\cref{sec:theory-equilibrium}), so every outcome below is a change in labor demand at a constant wage, not an equilibrium change in employment.
The magnitudes reflect this: in the Monte Carlo of \cref{sec:sim-mc} under the original specification, mean output rises by 61\% relative to the counterfactual (median 48\%), because adopters face neither a rising wage nor a falling output price as they expand (\texttt{data/monte\_carlo\_results.md}).
With finite labor supply or downward-sloping product demand, both gains and losses would be smaller.
Firms are classified by current size into the three classes of \cref{sec:theory-equilibrium} (fewer than 20, 20 to 499, and 500 or more employees).

\subsection{Substitutability versus shock size: normalizing the CES}\label{sec:sim-normalization}

A CES function has three parameters that matter here: the elasticity of substitution, the distribution of weight between inputs, and the efficiency (or price) of each input.
Comparing economies that differ in the elasticity alone requires holding the others fixed in an economically meaningful way; otherwise a change in the elasticity also changes the level of output or cost at the starting point, and its effect is confounded with a change in productivity \citep{klump2000growth,leonledesma2010identifying}.
The standard solution is to normalize the function at a baseline point and vary the elasticity holding that point fixed.

Earlier drafts did not normalize.
They held the reference AI price $\bar r$ fixed at 1.5 for every $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$, so $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ also set the size of the shock: at the reference point (full diffusion, exposure-one sector, $w = 1$), adoption cuts an adopter's unit cost by 69.7\% when $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 1.5$, by 11.65\% when $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3.5$, and by 4.4\% when $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$ (\texttt{data/normalization\_cost\_saving.md}).
A low elasticity was therefore also a very large cost shock, and a high elasticity a small one.
The single-firm headcount ratio $(c/w)^{\sigma_{\text{ai}} - \theta}$ of \cref{prop:threshold} shows the consequence: under the fixed price it falls from 0.960 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3$ to 0.900 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 4$ and then stops falling (0.899 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 4.5$, 0.905 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$), because the shrinking cost saving offsets the stronger substitution.

The \texttt{cost\_saving} normalization removes this confound.
For each $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ it sets the reference price $\bar r(\sigma_{\text{ai}})$ so that adoption at the reference point lowers the unit cost by the same 11.65\%, the saving the original specification delivers at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3.5$:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:normalization}
  c\bigl(1, \bar r(\sigma_{\text{ai}})\bigr) = 1 - 0.1165
  \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
  \bar r(\sigma_{\text{ai}}) = \bigl[(1 - 0.1165)^{1-\sigma_{\text{ai}}} - 1\bigr]^{\frac{1}{1-\sigma_{\text{ai}}}} .
\end{equation}
The two specifications therefore coincide at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3.5$.
Under the normalization, $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ changes only how easily AI replaces labor, and the single-firm headcount ratio $0.8835^{\sigma_{\text{ai}} - \theta}$ falls monotonically, from 1.171 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 1.5$ to 0.759 at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$.
Away from the reference point (other sectors, partial diffusion) the saving still varies with $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$, so the normalization fixes the size of the shock at one point rather than everywhere.

\paragraph{The regime map.}
\Cref{fig:regime-map} is the paper's central quantitative result.
It reports the mean net labor-demand effect, relative to the no-AI counterfactual, on a grid of $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ and the entrant productivity shift $\Delta\mu_e$, holding the entry-cost cut at 15\% and the adoption fixed cost at 6, with 10 seeds per cell.
These are comparative statics of the model, not probabilities.

\begin{result}[Regime map, numerical]\label{res:offset}
In the normalized specification (\cref{fig:regime-map}, right):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item[(i)] For every entrant productivity shift on the grid, the mean net labor-demand effect declines monotonically in $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$. With no entrant shift it falls from $+15.3\%$ at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 1.5$ to $-21.1\%$ at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$, crossing zero near $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3$ ($+0.1\%$, s.e.\ $1.7\%$), close to the single-firm threshold $\theta \approx 2.78$.
  \item[(ii)] Offset at high substitution requires more productive entrants, and the required shift rises with $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$: the effect changes sign between $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3.5$ and $4$ with $\Delta\mu_e = 0.05$, between $4$ and $4.5$ with $\Delta\mu_e = 0.10$, and between $4.5$ and $5$ with $\Delta\mu_e = 0.15$.
  \item[(iii)] At $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$ the effect is negative for every entrant shift on the grid, from $-21.1\%$ ($\Delta\mu_e = 0$) to $-3.3\%$ ($\Delta\mu_e = 0.15$).
\end{enumerate}
\end{result}

\noindent
The original specification (\cref{fig:regime-map}, left) tells a different story, and the difference is the confound described above.
At $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 1.5$ with no entrant shift, the net effect is $+373.9\%$, which reflects a 70\% cost cut rather than easy substitution.
At high $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ the effect flattens: with no entrant shift it is $-6.8\%$, $-7.2\%$, and $-7.1\%$ at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 4$, $4.5$, and $5$.
That plateau, which earlier drafts interpreted as offset ``levelling off'' rather than collapsing at high substitution, is an artifact of holding the AI price fixed: as $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ rises, the shrinking cost advantage of AI reduces adoption and so masks the stronger substitution.
With the cost saving held fixed, the plateau disappears, and at $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 5$ the net effect is $-21.1\%$ rather than $-7.1\%$ with no entrant shift.

The headline is therefore conditional and qualitative: \emph{offset fails when substitution is high and AI-native entrants are no more productive than earlier entrants.}
Where on the map the economy lies is unknown.
The threshold that separates the regions moves with $\alpha$ (\cref{tab:alpha-threshold}), the only available estimate of a related elasticity is below one \citep{luduvice2026ai}, and nothing in the paper measures the entrant productivity shift.
A companion note derives the industry-level offset condition analytically and shows the same trade-off between substitution and entrant quality.

\subsection{Robustness: Monte Carlo over an assumed prior}\label{sec:sim-mc}

Earlier drafts led with a Monte Carlo over priors for the four AI parameters and reported shares of draws as probabilities.
They are not probabilities about the world.
The priors in \cref{tab:calibration} are assumptions, chosen to straddle the threshold at $\alpha = 0.64$ rather than to match data, so a share of draws restates the prior as much as it measures the model.
We keep the Monte Carlo only as a robustness check on what drives variation within the assumed parameter space, and we report shares of draws under the assumed prior without confidence intervals.
Each run uses 2,000 draws with one simulation seed per draw and the same seed (2026) in both specifications.

\paragraph{Variance decomposition, original versus normalized.}
\Cref{tab:mc-normalized} reports first-order variance shares of the net labor-demand effect, $\mathrm{Var}(E[Y \mid X_i])/\mathrm{Var}(Y)$ estimated from 20 quantile bins, in the two specifications.
In the original specification, $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ accounts for 0.714 of the variance; with the cost saving held fixed it accounts for 0.618, and the entrant productivity shift rises from 0.187 to 0.250.
Some of $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$'s apparent importance therefore came from its role in scaling the shock, and entrant quality matters more once that role is removed.
The entry-cost reduction accounts for about 0.04 of the variance in both specifications.
First-order shares sum to less than one (0.946 and 0.920) because the parameters interact.
The interactions matter more for the sign of the effect than for its level: whether the net effect is positive depends on $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ and $\Delta\mu_e$ jointly, as the regime map shows, so a parameter with a modest first-order share can still decide the outcome in part of the space.

The by-$\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ profile in \cref{tab:mc-normalized} repeats the regime-map finding over the prior.
In the original specification, the share of draws with a positive net effect levels off at 68\% for $\sigma_{\text{ai}} \ge 4$; in the normalized specification it falls to 33\% for $\sigma_{\text{ai}} \in [4, 4.5)$ and 15\% for $\sigma_{\text{ai}} \in [4.5, 5)$, and the mean effect turns negative above $\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 4$.
Large-firm labor demand falls in 51\% of normalized draws against 44\% in the original.

\paragraph{Other prior choices (original specification).}
Two further checks were run on the original specification only.
Among draws in which large-firm labor demand falls, the share with a positive net effect is 59.2\% when the AI price is fixed and 44.5\% when it is drawn from a lognormal prior with median 1.5 and log standard deviation 0.4 (\texttt{data/monte\_carlo\_aicost\_results.md}); reweighting the draws to alternative priors on $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ over the same support moves it between 55.7\% and 63.3\%, and moves the unconditional share of draws with a positive net effect between 69.7\% and 89.0\% (\texttt{data/prior\_sensitivity.md}).
These are shares of draws under assumed priors, and the spread across priors is the point: they are not estimates of how likely offset is.
Both checks predate the normalization and have not been rerun under it.

\paragraph{Simulation noise.}
Each Monte Carlo draw uses a single simulation seed.
Sixty replications at fixed parameters ($\sigma_{\text{ai}} = 3.5$, a 20\% entry-cost cut, $\Delta\mu_e = 0.05$, $F_{\text{ai}} = 5$, original specification) give a net effect with standard deviation 4.2\%, about 6\% of the total variance across draws; for $\sigma_{\text{ai}} \ge 3.25$ noise is about a quarter of the variance, because the 500+ class holds few firms (\texttt{data/monte\_carlo\_results.md}).
A multi-seed Monte Carlo is part of the ongoing work.

\paragraph{Implications for monitoring.}
The regime map points to two quantities to monitor as AI diffuses:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Employment at AI-adopting firms relative to comparable non-adopters}, which bears on whether adopters are in the scale-dominant or substitution-dominant region. The BTOS AI supplement, in which about 2\% of AI-using firms reported AI-related employment reductions \citep{bonney2026}, records realized cuts at an early stage of diffusion; it does not measure $\sigma_{\text{ai}}$ or $\alpha$.
  \item \textbf{Productivity and employment at AI-native entrants}, that is, whether firms created with AI are more productive than counterfactual entrants and how many workers they employ per unit of output.
\end{enumerate}
