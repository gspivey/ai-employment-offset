\subsection{Employment-light AI firms}\label{sec:disc-applications}

If AI-induced formation offsets displacement, it must do so mainly through \emph{application} firms that build products on AI models, rather than through the frontier labs and cloud providers that supply them, whose growth is capital-intensive and whose employment lands largely at old, large firms.
The application firms that have scaled fastest are strikingly employment-light.
Cursor had roughly \$3.75 million of annualized revenue per employee in 2026 and Lovable about \$2.7 million, against about \$0.24 million at Snowflake at the end of its IPO fiscal year (\cref{tab:ai-vs-cloud} in \cref{app:company}).
Two cautions apply.
These comparisons set run-rate revenue at a point of very fast growth against a full year of reported revenue.
And revenue is not value added: application firms buy model access from AI providers, so part of their revenue pays for an input produced, and employing people, elsewhere.
A handful of firms is not systematic evidence.
The systematic evidence points the same way, however: AI-native startups are 12--25\% smaller in headcount than comparable firms \citep{kim2026ainative}, and \citet{bena2026prompted} find smaller entrants in exposed industries.
In the model, smaller entrants are what one expects when AI is also available to new firms (\cref{pred:entrant-size}); whether they add up to offset depends on how many there are and how fast they grow.

\subsection{More firms, not more employers}\label{sec:disc-augment}

Business applications in January--October 2026 were 10.9\% above the same months of 2025, and 36.9\% above in Information \citep{bfs2026}.
Applications with planned wages, a proxy for intent to become an employer, fell 16.5\% over the same period and fell in every one of the 19 sectors.
In Information, planned-wage applications were 12.3\% of all applications in 2019 and 4.7\% in January--October 2026 (\texttt{data/bfs\_revision\_numbers.md}).
Formation is rising at the firm-count margin but not at the employer margin, as in the event-study evidence of \cref{sec:sig-bfs}.
Because the decline in planned-wage applications appears in every sector, it is not specific to AI, and it continues a cloud-era trend, when such applications fell 48.1\% between 2006 and 2019 (\cref{sec:cloud-findings}).
The pattern is consistent with more but smaller firms, and also with the contractor-registration reading discussed in \cref{sec:sig-bfs}.

\subsection{Limitations and ongoing work}\label{sec:disc-limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{No equilibrium closure (ongoing).} The model holds the wage fixed and has no product-market interaction, so its outcomes are changes in labor demand, not employment. Closing it requires a finite labor-supply elasticity, downward-sloping product demand, and business stealing between adopters and their competitors, which firm-level studies of automation find to be quantitatively important \citep{acemoglu2020competing,koch2021robots}. Each would damp both gains and losses, and business stealing could make the aggregate effect of adoption smaller than the firm-level effect in \cref{prop:threshold}.
  \item \textbf{Not calibrated (ongoing).} The parameters are illustrative, and the pre-AI economy misses the data on the large-firm employment share by more than half (\cref{tab:moments}). A simulated-method-of-moments calibration to verified BDS and SUSB targets, with $\alpha$ estimated or varied rather than fixed at 0.64, is the next step.
  \item \textbf{Size classes and cohorts (ongoing).} The simulation classifies firms by current size at a 500-employee cutoff; the cohort-based offset ratio of \cref{def:offset} and alternative cutoffs have not been implemented.
  \item \textbf{Monte Carlo design (ongoing).} Each draw uses one simulation seed, and the AI-price and prior-reweighting checks have been run only on the unnormalized specification. A multi-seed Monte Carlo under the normalized CES is pending.
  \item \textbf{Cloud-era estimation (ongoing).} The exposure-based design of \cref{sec:cloud-strategy} has not been estimated, so the cloud era remains motivation.
  \item \textbf{Continuous exposure (ongoing).} The business-application evidence uses a binary classification of two sectors and is sensitive to that choice; a continuous exposure measure mapped to industries, and outcomes for employer entry rather than applications, are needed.
  \item \textbf{Elasticity support.} The parameter space requires $\sigma_{\text{ai}} > 1$, so complementarity between AI and labor, which the only related estimate suggests \citep{luduvice2026ai}, is excluded by construction.
  \item \textbf{Sector heterogeneity.} Sector exposures are assumed and fixed; offset could fail in highly exposed sectors and hold in others, and the model does not endogenize reallocation across sectors.
  \item \textbf{Job counts versus job quality.} The offset ratio counts jobs and is silent on wages, job security, and the matching of displaced workers to new firms.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Policy implications}\label{sec:disc-policy}

In this model, cutting the cost of entry changes where employment sits more than how much of it there is: across the specifications, the entry-cost reduction accounts for about 4\% of the variance in the net labor-demand effect (\cref{tab:mc-normalized}).
What decides the sign is whether adopters are in the substitution-dominant region and whether AI-native entrants are more productive (\cref{res:offset}).
Three implications follow, each conditional on the model's partial-equilibrium structure.
First, policies that only lower the cost of starting a business are unlikely to produce offset on their own if AI is strongly labor-substituting at adopters.
Second, the quantities worth measuring are labor demand at AI-adopting firms relative to comparable non-adopters and the productivity and employment of AI-native entrants; early survey evidence \citep{bonney2026} records few AI-related cuts so far but does not measure the substitution elasticity.
Third, the conditions for scale-up (finance, and access to AI services and compute on competitive terms) matter more for offset in this framework than the cost of registration, which is also the conclusion of the stylized European comparison in \cref{app:europe}.
